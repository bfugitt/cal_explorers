# Explorer's Choice: teacher notes

## Standards
- California HSS 4.2.3: Spanish and English exploration and colonization of California (including relationships among soldiers, missionaries, and Indians)
- HSS 4.1: geography of California (the coast, the Pacific Ocean, Baja California)
- Analysis skills: chronological and spatial thinking, and point of view

## How the game works
Students choose an explorer (there are seven) and follow a voyage along a map. At each stop they read a short scene and make a choice. Their choices affect five meters:
supplies, crew health, morale, trust of local people, and the ship (or, for Portolá, the animals and gear).
If supplies or crew health reach zero, the expedition must turn back. There are also camps, where students choose how to use their time, and random hazards.

**Change history.** Many choices are what the real explorer did (marked "✔ This is what the real explorer did").
If a student picks something else, a popup card explains what the real explorer actually did. The other outcomes are labeled "What might have happened," because no one can know.
At the end, students see how many times they matched history, what really happened, and the hardships they faced.

## The seven voyages
| Explorer | Goal (from the chapter) | Hardships | Real choices students can match |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cabrillo** (Spain, 1542), Medium | Follow the coast, claim land, look for treasure, search for a waterway inland | Wind and current, fog and rocks, few harbors, people who were afraid, infection | Hug the coast; claim land; row ashore to fish; sail on to Oregon after Cabrillo's death |
| **Drake** (England, 1577 to 1580), Medium | Take Spanish treasure, find the Northwest Passage, get home | Storms, a heavy leaking ship, Spanish ships, a long voyage home | Keep the queen's secret; push on alone; attack Spanish ships; take all the treasure; search for the passage; repair at a bay; make the brass plate; sail west |
| **Vizcaíno** (Spain, 1602), Medium | Map the coast and find harbors | Winds and currents; six months to reach San Diego; winning the king's interest | Sail on north after San Diego; call Monterey Bay the perfect harbor |
| **Bering** (Russia, 1728 and 1741), Medium | Find out if Asia and North America are connected; later, map North America's coast | Storms, fog, arctic ice, illness | Report the strait and turn back; map the coast in 1741; explore the islands; turn back from storms and illness |
| **Portolá** (Spain, 1769), Hard | Start settlements at San Diego and Monterey | Hilly desert, sick groups from the ships, finding Monterey | Go by land; leave Serra with the sick; accept the Chumash gift; keep going past Monterey |
| **Anza** (Spain, 1775), Medium | Lead settlers to Monterey and choose a site for a mission at San Francisco Bay | Desert and mountains, feeding settlers and animals, the Yuma villages | Bring a large group of settlers; go north to San Francisco Bay; after the 1781 attack, stop using the overland route |
| **Cook** (England, 1778), Medium | Search from Oregon to Alaska for the Northwest Passage | Fog and storms, arctic ice, distance | Explore the coast from Oregon north; sail through the Bering Strait; turn back from the ice |

Cook, Bering, and Drake all searched for the Northwest Passage. Students can compare how their searches were alike.

## Things to check
1. **Local people.** The Kumeyaay, Chumash, and Miwok appear as people with their own choices, using only what the chapter says. A "Trust" meter rewards respectful choices, and no option rewards taking by force.
2. **Cabrillo's death** is told simply, as in the chapter. Students then lead the crew. Please check that this suits your class.
3. **Drake's attacks.** The chapter describes them. A "Read more" note says that to the Spanish this was stealing.
4. **Alternate outcomes** (for example, what might have happened if Portolá had settled at Monterey right away) are my own guesses and are labeled as such. Monterey did turn out to be the right place, but the game does not claim to know what would have followed.
5. **Bering and Cook.** The chapter says Bering and Cook died before they could return home or search again. The game tells this the way the chapter does. The chapter says nothing about the Native peoples of Alaska and Siberia, so those voyages have no "people on shore" scenes. A class discussion could note whose stories are missing.
6. **Anza and the Yuma.** The chapter says the settlers' animals trampled the Yuma's crops and that Yumas attacked settlers in 1781. The game lets students treat the Yuma fairly, but then says what really happened. The result of fair dealing is labeled a guess.
7. **Vizcaíno.** Calling Monterey Bay "all that one could hope for" connects to Portolá, whose men could not recognize the bay from that description.
8. **Details outside the chapter:** route lines on the maps are approximate, and Portolá's starting point in Baja California is shown generally because the chapter does not name a town.
9. **Game numbers.** Supplies, health, and other values are game values, not historical measurements.

## Reading support
- Short scenes with pictures and emoji, and a "Read more" box for extra detail.
- The Voyage log lets students review each choice and whether it matched history.
- Changing history never "wins" or "loses" by itself. Students can learn from either path.

## Discussion questions
1. How were the goals of Cabrillo, Portolá, and Drake different? How were their hardships different?
2. Which choices did you change? What does the real explorer's choice tell us about what he knew and wanted?
3. How did the Kumeyaay, Chumash, and Miwok respond to the explorers? Why?
4. Whose point of view is missing from each story?
